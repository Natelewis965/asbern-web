One link, no account

Three taps from “want to watch?” to watching.

The friction in every other approach is the account. Plex wants a login. A screen-share wants everyone in one voice channel on a desktop client. Asbern hands out a link.

  1. Someone starts a screening
    From the menu: pick a film, an episode or a channel. You have to be in a voice channel to start one, and that voice channel's screen is the one you are steering — so two groups can never fight over the same picture.
  2. Everyone taps Watch
    Each tap mints a single-use link for that person. It opens in a browser. There is no app, no login, and no “can someone invite me”.
  3. The room knows who arrived
    Because the link is per-person and the stream is authorised on every segment, presence is measured at the player. That drives watch-time payouts, resume points, the end-of-film roster, the auto-advance to the next episode, and the fifteen-minute empty-room shutdown.

Two screens, independently

A second screen is an admin toggle with its own engine and its own public link, so two Discord categories can watch different things at the same time. Each screen plays one thing at a time — there is deliberately no queue. The controls on a now-playing card carry their own screen's id, so Stop can never hit the wrong picture.

It cleans up after itself

A series advances episode to episode while anyone is still watching. A film ends where it ends. Either way the screen shuts off after fifteen minutes with nobody on it, and the channel keeps exactly one live now-playing card rather than a graveyard of dead embeds.

“Tell me when it lands”

Ask for something that is not in the library and Asbern remembers — across restarts. When it arrives, everyone who asked gets pinged with a one-tap start button. For a series that fires the moment S1E1 exists, not when the whole run is there.

Asbern reads your library. It is never wired to acquisition — no Radarr, no Sonarr, no torrent client, by design.

The unglamorous part

What this actually needs from your machine.

The transcode has to sit next to your files, so it runs on your hardware. That means the encoder question is real, and we would rather answer it here than in a support ticket at nine on a Friday.

Intel QuickSync does not work inside Docker on Windows or WSL2. This is measured, not assumed — it is what cancelled an entire hardware migration plan on our own deployment. On Windows you want NVENC. On Linux, QuickSync is fine with device passthrough.

Why the relay exists

Your upload speed is the real ceiling.

A stream is cheap to make and expensive to hand out. Twenty viewers at five megabits is a hundred megabits of upstream, and almost no residential line has that. This is the single technical reason the hosted relay is the piece we charge for.

Without a relay
100 Mbps

Your machine sends the whole stream to every single viewer. Twenty people at 5 Mbps each. Your upstream is saturated, everything else on your network suffers, and the twenty-first person cannot join.

With the relay
8 Mbps

Your machine pushes one stream up. We fan it out. Twenty viewers, two hundred viewers — your upstream does not change, and neither does your Friday night.

The bridge, in one paragraph

A cloud service cannot reach into a home network — NAT, dynamic addresses, no port forwarding, and asking people to open ports is both a support burden and a genuine security liability. So the agent on your machine dials out and holds the connection open, the same way a CI runner does. It declares what it can do; the platform may never ask for more than it declared. If it vanishes mid-operation, the answer your members see is “offline” — a stated condition, never a guess that something is fine.

Planned The agent pattern is in production today, but it currently dials inward across a LAN. The outbound bridge and the signed installer are on the roadmap and are not shipped. See what is and is not built.

Questions people actually ask

Screening room FAQ

Does Asbern host or provide any content?
No. Asbern reads a Plex server that you run, and an IPTV source that you supply. It has no library of its own, no acquisition tooling, and no integration with any downloader — that separation is deliberate and it is enforced in the code, not in a policy.
Do viewers need Plex, or an account of any kind?
No. A viewer needs a browser and the link the bot gave them. That is the whole point — the people you most want at a watch party are the ones who will not install anything.
How many people can watch at once?
Through the relay, more than a Discord server realistically needs; the limit is your plan's included viewer-hours and then metered overage. Without the relay, your upload speed divided by about five megabits.
Is it synchronised, or does everyone drift?
It is a broadcast, not a set of independent playbacks — everyone is receiving the same live stream, so there is nothing to drift out of sync. The trade-off is the other way around: nobody can pause it for themselves, because there is only one picture.
What happens if a live channel dies mid-stream?
It is caught two ways — it never comes up, or it drops partway — and both do the same three things: log it, tell the room it has been flagged, and sweep the stale card. Repeat offenders accumulate a count that moderators can see.
Can I run this without a GPU?
Yes, and it will work — software transcode costs roughly four to six CPU cores per concurrent stream. On a machine that is also hosting game servers, that is the difference between a quiet evening and a loud one.
